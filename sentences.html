The sentence shift

50 of the 51 source-supported terms in this file, plotted against the chapter they belong to. Red is imprisonment, amber is home detention, the pale line marks the middle of each chapter's prison terms. A dot's height is nothing — only its position on the axis.

What this shows

This is a record of what courts did in the cases this file could source, not a sentencing survey. The chapters are uneven — one holds two reported terms, another holds sixteen — so the shape of a row says as much about how much was reported as about how hard the sentence was.

Two Whoosh courier terms are left out of the plot because two reports disagree on each of them; the chapter shows both figures and prefers neither. Rong-Jun Sui, 9 years 4 months is left out because the cited article does not pin the year of the sentencing. Home detention sits on the same axis as imprisonment but is prison's alternative, not its equal. And a dot is a sentence, not a verdict: the 13 months term at the bottom of the Avon row is a cocaine possession sentence — the man it belongs to was acquitted on 25 other charges, and the chapter prints that acquittal in full.

What moved

computed from the plotted terms
12 yearsthe longest term in the 2023—26 cases

The ceiling in the 2004—09 cases was 8 years, for the woman convicted over the Tsunami conspiracy. In 2023—26 the top term is 12 years — the head of the Whoosh import ring. The register files 7 hundred-month-plus terms in the 2023—26 group and 2 before it.

5y 9mmedian prison term, 2023—26

The middle of the range sat at 3y 6m and 3y 10m through 2013, stepped up to 5y 9m in 2019—20, and has held near there at 5y 9m in 2023—26. Most people sentenced in these operations were never at the top of the pyramid.

5 of 30terms that were home detention, 2023—26

Home detention is now a routine bottom-tier outcome: 5 of the 30 plotted terms in 2023—26, against 1 of 9 in 2010—13. The floor of the record got softer while the ceiling rose.

What this cannot tell you

It cannot tell you whether sentences got harsher, because the cases are not comparable. A 2004 charge of running a portable lab in a suburban house and a 2026 charge of running an import ring through a dark-web shop are different offences with different maxima, different volumes and different roles. What the record shows is what was imposed in each case, in the order the cases appear.

It cannot tell you about the sentences that were never reported. 24 case records in this file carry a conviction, a plea or a charge without a recovered term — Norwich Street in 1990 has three guilty pleas and no reported sentence at all. Those gaps are not zeros and they are not acquittals.

One name appears more than once in this table — Andrew Michael Smith (2 rows). Those are different people, not one man sentenced twice — the chapter numbers differ, and the register carries a note on each name trap.

And it cannot tell you the sentence that was later reduced on appeal. Where an appeal outcome is in the register — Maddale, Tsunami, Whoosh — the chapter says so; the plot shows the term as reported at the point the source describes it.
